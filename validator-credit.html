Self-Repaying Validator Credit

Validators sell reward FLR to pay their bills — a sell flow working against the FIRE buyback. Flare has already shipped the building blocks to replace that selling with credit. This is the design.

CLAIMS CHECKED AGAINST CHAIN AND SOURCE · 2026-08-26

01 · The problem

Operators sell to pay the bills

Every validator has real costs — servers, bandwidth, time — and an operator without outside income has one obvious way to fund them: selling reward FLR on-market. In April 2026, governance passed FIP.16 with 98.06% support: FIRE buys back and burns FLR, and inflation drops from 5% to 3%. Operator selling works against that every epoch.

Telling operators "just don't sell" isn't a mechanism. Giving them something cheaper than selling is — and it can be assembled almost entirely from contracts already live on Flare mainnet.

02 · The insight

The pieces already exist on Flare

A track record nobody can fake, published by the network itself. Built-in permission for a wallet to let a contract collect its rewards and pass them along. A price feed run by the validators themselves. Everything a lender needs — proof of income, a way to get repaid, and a fair price — already lives on-chain.

03 · The mechanism

The loan that repays itself

How much can you borrow?

Up to 70% of what your rewards are on track to earn during the loan — but never more than half the value of the collateral you put up. The first cushion covers bad weeks; the second covers price drops. (On fixed-FLR loans, debt and collateral are both FLR — that ratio cannot be moved by price.)
line = min( 70% × trailing rewards × term , 50% × collateral value )

Underwriting — the pass ledger is a credit score

Flare publishes every validator's report card — passes, strikes, uptime, eligibility — every 3.5 days, where anyone can check it. Loan terms read straight from that report card:

TierChain-verifiable requirementTerms
A≥3M self-bond · ≥15M stake · 3/3 passesLowest rate, highest advance, smallest collateral
BPass-eligible · 1–2 passesStandard
C≥1M self-bond · reward-eligible · 0 passesSmall line, heavy collateral
—Recent strikesNo loan until the record heals
The floating pass-rate

Interest reprices every epoch from the borrower's live pass count — as a spread over the flavor's benchmark: base is roughly benchmark + 4 points, minus 1 point per pass held, so a 3/3-pass borrower pays benchmark + 1 — the cheapest money in the system, but never below what the lender could earn passively. (Benchmarks: the staking yield for fixed-FLR loans; stablecoin yield for fixed-dollar loans.) A strike drifts the rate up until the record heals. In short: the better you run your validator, the cheaper your money — recalculated every 3.5 days from public records. It also hands validators a direct cash incentive to maintain exactly the discipline the network already wants.

No liquidation price — only a dead validator defaults

What about the self-bond?

The self-bond looks like perfect collateral — millions of FLR, provably locked, readable live from the chain. The catch: it sits on the P-chain, which has no smart contracts, and when the bond ends it returns automatically to its owner. A lender can see it but can never take it. So the design uses it three ways:

  • As a qualification. The top tier requires ≥3M self-bond and ≥15M stake — the same bar Flare itself sets for earning passes.
  • As a promise with teeth (today). The loan requires keeping the self-bond and pass status intact for its whole life. Dropping either counts the same as a dead income stream — it starts the default clock.
  • As true collateral (with one small protocol addition). Let a validator declare, at any point during the bond, where the stake pays out when it ends — releasable only by the named lender until the loan clears. That single change makes the most locked asset on the network usable as hard collateral, and loans backed by it need far less collateral and cost less. Existing bonds could enroll mid-term, so nobody waits out a 365-day bond to qualify.

Even then, a default takes only what's owed — the rest of the bond comes back to its owner. That rule is what keeps the whole system honest:

Default is a settlement, not a jackpot

On default the vault takes exactly the balance, accrued interest, and a fixed default fee. Every remaining token returns to the borrower. Lenders are assured recovery up to the collateral's value, never enrichment — so no party ever profits from causing a borrower to fail. This forecloses the loan-to-own attack class by construction, and it is why the credit prices cheap: near-certain recovery prices like secured lending, not venture debt.

04 · Lender economics

Why lenders show up

Updated Aug 27 — after pushback, and the pushback was right

The first version of this section gave lenders a dollar-based claim, repaid in FLR at delivery prices — which quietly capped their upside: if FLR rose, the lender missed most of the ride. A reader called it out. The fix is better than a patch: two loan flavors, and the lender picks their risk.

Every loan is written in one of two flavors, chosen when it opens:

Fixed-FLR loans (the new default)

The borrower owes a fixed number of FLR, set by the oracle price on day one. The lender's return: full FLR upside plus interest — strictly better than buying the same FLR on the market, since coins you hold pay nothing. Their risk: FLR's price, same as any holder. And because the borrower's income is also FLR, their repayment schedule is price-proof — the same number of epochs whatever the price does — and the coverage between FLR debt and FLR collateral can't be moved by price at all. The borrower's trade: they've effectively sold those coins forward at today's price — off-market, so the network still sees zero sell pressure — and they give up the upside on the repaid coins. In exchange: the cheapest rates in the system.

Fixed-dollar loans

The borrower owes a dollar amount, repaid in FLR valued at each epoch's oracle price. The borrower keeps all the upside — a rising price means fewer coins to repay — so lenders charge a higher rate for accepting the cap, and in return get real downside protection: falling prices deliver them more coins per dollar owed, and collateral settles any default, up to its value.

What sets the rate?

The staking yield. No FLR holder lends below what staking pays them risk-adjusted, so each flavor clears above its own lender's alternative: fixed-FLR loans above the staking yield, fixed-dollar loans above stablecoin yield — with the pass discount moving borrowers along that spread, never below it. What the lender buys with that spread: a fixed, collateral-secured return instead of staking's variable, performance-dependent one (a validator's strike epoch pays stakers zero; the loan keeps accruing). Borrowers can afford staking-plus because they aren't buying leverage — they're buying time: income moved forward into a lump sum, for a few hundred dollars of interest, without a coin touching the order book. And no capital idles: each FLR tranche a lender receives is wrapped and delegated the day it lands, earning from delivery.

Bulls borrow in dollars and lend in FLR; cautious capital does the reverse. The rate gap between the two flavors is set by whoever shows up — a market discovering the price of upside, not a fixed product.

05 · Stated limitations

What we haven't solved

Seven known limitations, and what cures each
  1. The income pledge isn't ironclad on normal wallets. Owners can always self-claim around the vault, so v1 is honestly a collateral-backed loan whose normal path is self-repayment — the innovation is the UX and the underwriting data. Cured in v2 by claim-binding accounts.
  2. Everyone's rewards can change at once. A network-wide reward change shrinks every stream at once — FIP.16's accepted restructuring is reshaping reward economics now, and future governance can again. On fixed-dollar loans that stretches coverage; on fixed-FLR loans it stretches time — the same coins owed take more epochs to earn. Mitigated by short loans that renew at current reward levels, a reserve fund, and cautious loan sizing.
  3. Rewards expire if nobody claims them (~90 days). An automated claimer must run on schedule, with a backup. Structurally solved by v2 enrollment.
  4. The market starts small. Flare's validator set alone is a niche book — the cross-chain roadmap is where the market becomes large.
  5. The insurance layer is new. Cover terms must be pre-agreed with Firelight (the on-Flare insurance market this design would buy cover from); open design item, not a solved one.
  6. Two flavors need two crowds. Rates here are discovered, not set — and a young market with few lenders and few borrowers may not discover them cleanly at first. Early loans will likely need hand-matched or anchor capital before the spread between the flavors settles.
  7. Lending has legal weight. The operator needs real counsel — and CashLab is explicitly not volunteering to operate or custody.

06 · Roadmap

The road from here

Why this serves Flare

  • Deletes operator sell pressure at the source — the complement to FIRE: revenue removes FLR; this keeps operator flow from ever reaching the market.
  • Pays validators to maintain pass discipline via the floating rate.
  • Creates organic premium demand for Firelight's cover market.
  • Gives FAssets and the FDC a concrete use case.
  • Retains small validators — the operators for whom server-bills-versus-selling is a monthly dilemma.
