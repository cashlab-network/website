In 60 seconds

The whole idea, before the deep dive

Borrow against the rewards you haven't earned yet — and let them repay the loan for you. If you earn FLR rewards — as a validator or a staker — you can borrow stablecoins against them. Every epoch, your rewards go straight to the lender until the loan is paid off. Your bills get paid in dollars. Not one coin is sold on-market.

Two doors, one rule. Door one: validators and P-chain stakers — the chain itself shows their stake is locked, and the stake must outlive the debt. Door two: everyday FLR holders — their collateral is delegated inside an escrow, and its own earnings repay the loan; it can't be unplugged, and it all comes back at payoff. The rule both doors share: we only lend against streams that can't run away. Freely movable tokens don't qualify.

Your record is your rate. Flare grades every operator with its pass system (0–3 passes). A validator's own passes set their rate — and a staker inherits the passes of the validator they stake with, so choosing a reliable operator earns you cheaper money. There is no liquidation price: a market crash cannot liquidate anyone. Only a genuinely dead stream, or a loan still unpaid at its deadline, can default — and settlement takes exactly what's owed, returning everything else.

Where it stands. A working prototype runs on Flare's Coston2 testnet: complete loans of both types, rewards claimed through Flare's real reward machinery, both borrower doors built and tested, 133 automated tests (including a high-volume scenario + fuzz suite that runs ~80 deterministic loans and, at full fuzz depth, 10,000 randomized ones through the full lifecycle, each conserving to the wei), and five adversarial AI red-team reviews — every HIGH and MEDIUM finding fixed, with the accepted low-severity residuals documented. The next gate is a formal audit — no real funds before it. Everything below is the full design, risks included.

01 · The problem

People sell their rewards to pay the bills

A validator has real costs — servers, bandwidth, time — and an operator without outside income funds them one obvious way: selling reward FLR on-market. A staker has the same instinct on a smaller scale: rent is due this month, the rewards trickle in over many, so they sell. Both are the same sell flow, and in April 2026 governance voted to fight exactly it — FIP.16 passed with 98.06% support: FIRE buys back and burns FLR, inflation drops from 5% to 3%. Every reward sold works against that.

Telling people "just don't sell" isn't a mechanism. Giving them something cheaper than selling is — and it can be assembled almost entirely from contracts already live on Flare mainnet.

02 · The insight

The pieces already exist on Flare

A track record nobody can fake, published by the network itself. Built-in permission for a wallet to let a contract collect its rewards and pass them along. A price feed run by the validators themselves. Everything a lender needs — proof of income, a way to get repaid, and a fair price — already lives on-chain.

03 · The mechanism

The loan that repays itself

How much can you borrow?

Up to 70% of what your rewards are on track to earn during the loan — but never more than half the value of the collateral you put up. The first cushion covers bad weeks; the second covers price drops. (On fixed-FLR loans, debt and collateral are both FLR — that ratio cannot be moved by price.)
line = min( 70% × trailing rewards × term , 50% × collateral value )

Underwriting — the pass ledger is a credit score

Flare publishes every validator's report card — passes, strikes, uptime, eligibility — every 3.5 days, where anyone can check it. A validator's loan terms read straight from that record; a staker inherits the record of the validator they stake with, so who you delegate to is who underwrites you:

TierChain-verifiable requirementTerms
A3/3 passes (your own, or your validator's)Lowest rate, highest advance, thinnest margin
B1–2 passes · reward-eligibleStandard
C0 passes · reward-eligibleSmall line, heavier margin
—Recent strikesNo loan until the record heals
The floating pass-rate

Interest reprices every epoch from the borrower's live pass count — as a spread over that loan type's benchmark: base is roughly benchmark + 4 points, minus 1 point per pass held, so a 3/3-pass borrower pays benchmark + 1 — the cheapest money in the system, but never below what the lender could earn passively. (Benchmarks: the staking yield for fixed-FLR loans; stablecoin yield for fixed-dollar loans.) A strike drifts the rate up until the record heals. In short: the better you run your validator, the cheaper your money — recalculated every 3.5 days from public records. It also hands validators a direct cash incentive to maintain exactly the discipline the network already wants.

No liquidation price — defaults are states, not prices

What secures a loan — collateral vs. commitment

Two different things protect the lender, and it's worth keeping them straight:

  • The collateral is the posted margin — the same for everyone, and it never sits idle. Every borrower, validator or staker, locks wrapped FLR in the loan's own escrow — and the escrow immediately delegates it, so the collateral keeps earning every epoch it's pledged (on Flare, wrapped-and-delegated is the earning form; naked FLR earns nothing). That margin is what settlement seizes on default — the lender's guaranteed recovery. A regular staker is not unsecured: their escrow-locked, still-earning stake is that margin.
  • The commitment is the stream that can't run away. Two ways to prove it: a validator's live P-chain stake (Flare's own stake mirror shows it on-chain — if it ever disappears while you owe, anyone can start the default clock), or an everyday holder's escrow-locked delegation (the escrow holds the stake, so you literally can't unplug the stream). This is what qualifies you to borrow; it is not what the lender takes. A stream you could freely switch off — plain tokens you haven't committed — doesn't qualify at all: this market only lends against income that can't run away.

So is the self-bond needed? No. It sits on the P-chain, which has no smart contracts, so a lender could always see it but never seize it — the margin does the securing instead. The self-bond stays useful only as a strength signal (a validator with millions locked is a safer borrower) and as an optional future efficiency: an opt-in P-chain feature (roadmap v3) could let a staker mark a stake borrowable at creation, so its returned principal can back a loan trustlessly — turning the stake itself into hard collateral and cutting the margin they'd need. A real feature, not a one-liner, and never required — the product works fully today without it.

On default, either way, only what's owed is taken — the rest returns to the borrower. That rule keeps the whole system honest:

Default is a settlement, not a jackpot

On default the vault takes exactly the balance, accrued interest, and a fixed default fee. Every remaining token returns to the borrower. Lenders are assured recovery up to the collateral's value, never enrichment — so no party ever profits from causing a borrower to fail. This forecloses the loan-to-own attack class by construction, and it is why the credit prices cheap: near-certain recovery prices like secured lending, not venture debt.

04 · Lender economics

Why lenders show up

Updated Aug 27 — after pushback, and the pushback was right

The first version of this section gave lenders a dollar-based claim, repaid in FLR at delivery prices — which quietly capped their upside: if FLR rose, the lender missed most of the ride. A reader called it out. The fix is better than a patch: two loan types, and the lender picks their risk.

Every loan is one of two types — the difference is simply what currency you owe — chosen when it opens:

Fixed-FLR loans (the new default)

The borrower owes a fixed number of FLR, set by the oracle price on day one. The lender's return: full FLR upside plus interest — strictly better than buying the same FLR on the market, since coins you hold pay nothing. Their risk: FLR's price, same as any holder. And because the borrower's income is also FLR, their repayment schedule is price-proof — the same number of epochs whatever the price does — and the coverage between FLR debt and FLR collateral can't be moved by price at all. The borrower's trade: they've effectively sold those coins forward at today's price — off-market, so the network still sees zero sell pressure — and they give up the upside on the repaid coins. In exchange: the cheapest rates in the system.

Fixed-dollar loans

The borrower owes a dollar amount, repaid in FLR valued at each epoch's oracle price. The borrower keeps all the upside — a rising price means fewer coins to repay — so lenders charge a higher rate for accepting the cap, and in return get real downside protection: falling prices deliver them more coins per dollar owed, and collateral settles any default, up to its value.

What sets the rate?

The staking yield. No FLR holder lends below what staking pays them risk-adjusted, so each loan type clears above its own lender's alternative: fixed-FLR loans above the staking yield, fixed-dollar loans above stablecoin yield — with the pass discount moving borrowers along that spread, never below it. What the lender buys with that spread: a fixed, collateral-secured return instead of staking's variable, performance-dependent one (a validator's strike epoch pays stakers zero; the loan keeps accruing). Borrowers can afford staking-plus because they aren't buying leverage — they're buying time: income moved forward into a lump sum, for a few hundred dollars of interest, without a coin touching the order book. And no capital idles: each FLR tranche a lender receives is wrapped and delegated the day it lands, earning from delivery.

Bulls borrow in dollars and lend in FLR; cautious capital does the reverse. The rate gap between the two types is set by whoever shows up — a market discovering the price of upside, not a fixed product.

05 · Stated limitations

What we haven't solved

Seven known limitations, and what cures each
  1. The income pledge on plain wallets — now largely closed. A plain wallet can always self-claim around the vault. Two fixes are now built: for escrow-locked delegations the stream is ironclad by construction (the escrow holds the stake, so there's nothing to claim around); for wallet-based borrowers, the claim-binding account is built and tested — bound to a loan, it physically cannot move value anywhere but the loan. What remains is real-world adoption of that account.
  2. Everyone's rewards can change at once. A network-wide reward change shrinks every stream at once — FIP.16's accepted restructuring is reshaping reward economics now, and future governance can again. On fixed-dollar loans that stretches coverage; on fixed-FLR loans it stretches time — the same coins owed take more epochs to earn. Mitigated by short loans that renew at current reward levels, a reserve fund, and cautious loan sizing.
  3. Rewards expire if nobody claims them (~90 days). An automated claimer must run on schedule, with a backup. The escrow now enrolls its own claims automatically, and the executor claim path is proven — a real reward has been claimed through it on testnet. What remains is keeper redundancy at production scale.
  4. The market starts small. Flare's validator set alone is a niche book. The staker door (added 08-28) broadens it to every committed FLR staker and escrow-locked delegation — and the cross-chain roadmap is where it becomes large.
  5. The insurance layer is new. Cover terms must be pre-agreed with Firelight (the on-Flare insurance market this design would buy cover from); open design item, not a solved one.
  6. Two loan types need two crowds. Rates here are discovered, not set — and a young market with few lenders and few borrowers may not discover them cleanly at first. Early loans will likely need hand-matched or anchor capital before the spread between the two types settles.
  7. Lending has legal weight. The operator needs real counsel — and CashLab is explicitly not volunteering to operate or custody.

06 · Roadmap

The road from here

Why this serves Flare

  • Deletes operator sell pressure at the source — the complement to FIRE: revenue removes FLR; this keeps operator flow from ever reaching the market.
  • Pays validators to maintain pass discipline via the floating rate.
  • Creates organic premium demand for Firelight's cover market.
  • Gives FAssets and the FDC a concrete use case.
  • Retains small validators — the operators for whom server-bills-versus-selling is a monthly dilemma.
